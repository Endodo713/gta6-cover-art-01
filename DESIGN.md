# Design Philosophy — "Neon Vice"

## The movement

**Neon Vice** — a poster language built from one idea: a city that only
exists after sunset. Everything in it is lit by signs, not by the sun.
It is a movement of heat, chrome, and hard edges, made for a single
glance from across the room.

## Space and form

The canvas is cut, never decorated. Photos live inside bold panels
separated by clean, unapologetic dividers — the grid is the frame, and
the frame is part of the picture. One panel is always the hero and takes
at least half the canvas; the rest are supporting shots, stacked or
paired, never competing. Nothing floats: every element is anchored to
an edge or a divider, and the empty space is the dark sky between the
signs.

## Color and material

The grade is the whole identity: magenta bleeding into hot orange,
cut by electric cyan, all laid over near-black. The photos are graded
toward that sunset — warm shadows, magenta mid-tones, a cyan kiss on
the highlights — so any photo, from any phone, belongs to the same
city. Type is white or chrome on the dark, never grey on grey; the
palette is three hues plus black and white, and nothing else is allowed
in.

## Scale and rhythm

One size does the shouting. The caption is set enormous — a single
line of condensed capitals that spans the width — and everything else
whispers: a small letterspaced kicker, a thin date line, nothing more.
The rhythm is 3:1: the hero caption is at least three times the size
of any secondary text, so the eye lands once, hard, and then rests.
Panels repeat the same divider weight so the eye moves in a steady
beat instead of stumbling.

## Composition and balance

The composition is a stack with a punchline. The photo panels carry
the top two-thirds; the caption owns the bottom third like a
billboard. Weight sits low and wide, the sky sits high and dark, and
a single diagonal — a sun, a horizon, a palm line — breaks the grid
just enough to keep it alive. Balance comes from contrast, not
symmetry: a big dark mass against one hot strip of light.

## Visual hierarchy

Three levels, no more. Level one: the caption, the only thing that
must survive a 120-pixel thumbnail. Level two: the hero panel, the
face of the cover. Level three: the supporting panels and the kicker.
If a fourth level appears, something gets deleted. The squint test
decides: blur the cover until the words die, and one bright shape —
the caption block against the dark — must still be the only thing
you see.

## Craft

Every edge is deliberate: dividers exactly even, caption tracked
tight, grade applied evenly across every panel so the cover reads as
one photograph of one city, not a collage. The work is original
throughout — drawn gradients, a condensed display face under the SIL
Open Font License, and the user's own photos. No borrowed logo, no
borrowed artwork, no borrowed type.
